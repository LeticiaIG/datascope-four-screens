# DataScope · Four Screens

Prototype that tells the data-center story on four screens (three walls and a table), alongside the MIT City Science [DataScope](https://cityscope.media.mit.edu/DataScope/) tool.

Live: https://leticiaig.github.io/datascope-four-screens/

For a case study that has no original DataScope (Texas), the left wall is a sentiment map of reported positions that stays open for the room, and the center wall is the real place, in two views. **Ground** (the default) puts the eye where the listener stands, looking at the plant: the land is the aerial photograph (Esri World Imagery) lying flat, so trees and roofs are seen as they are from the air; buildings stand up where OpenStreetMap has them, the belts planted on the table stand up as trees, and the sound is drawn as rings on the ground, one for every 5 dBA, from the same calculation as the readout. Tap the ground to walk there, drag to look around, use the wheel or the arrows to walk and turn; the eye can be set 2, 10, 30 or 80 m above the ground. The sky, its haze and the clouds are scenery. **Above** is the axonometric, drawn in the manner of the original's Site layout (pale ground, light roads, pastel blocks, the 45 and 55 dBA contours in mauve) but not cut around the site: the ground, roads and buildings are OpenStreetMap's and carry on in every direction. Residents (2020 Census blocks, one dot for a fixed number of people) move as dots colored by what they would hear, and can be switched off. A figure marks where you stand: drag it, or tap the ground, and the readout and the sound follow. **Immersive** fills the screen, hides the bar and moves the view slowly (a look from side to side on the ground, a turn from above); H hides the panels.

On the Texas table, under Design, the heights of the D2 berm and the D3 walls can be set, and trees, walls and berms that are in none of the four designs can be added with two taps. They enter the sound where you stand and the residents' dots.

**The campus (Texas).** The data center is drawn as a campus: two data halls on a service spine with the cooling units on their roofs, an equipment yard to the west, a substation, an office, a car park and a fence. It is a model made for this page, in the page's own style. Only the outline of the halls (90 m by 46.45 m), the yard and the heights the sound model uses come from the original DataScope; the generators, the substation and the rest are drawn to be recognised, not taken from a plan. The four designs change the same campus: D2 adds the berm and the belt of trees, D3 moves the coolers and the generators into a yard behind sound walls, D4 adds the district. The cooling packages change the roof units (more and taller for dry cooling), add the water tank (T3) or the heat pumps (T4).

**Energy on the campus.** With the grid alone (E1) nothing is drawn. A chosen solar or wind supply would be built elsewhere; it is drawn on the ground north of the fence, and named as built elsewhere, so that what the budget buys can be seen: as many solar tables or turbines as the share of the power it pays for (the same rule as before: 42 tables or 5 turbines at most). Raise the budget on the table and more appear.

**Immersive: the parts of the campus.** In the immersive view each part of the campus carries a dot (data halls, cooling, equipment yard or sound walls, substation, and what the choices add: solar, wind or microreactors, the berm, the district, heat recovery). Tap a dot to read what the part is and what the choices made change there. "Go there" walks to a place beside it and turns to face it, and Next goes round the campus part by part; the listener goes along, so the sound and its readout follow. Every figure in these cards is one the page shows elsewhere, from the same source. The idea of visiting a data center part by part comes from virtual data center tours; nothing of theirs is used.

**Linked maps (Texas).** The table, the sentiment map and the center wall show the same place. Moving any of the maps by hand (a drag, a zoom) moves the others, and the listener travels the same step, so the wall seen from the ground follows while the map is still being dragged and keeps looking at what the table is centred on. Moving only the listener (dragging the figure, tapping the ground, walking on the wall) changes where the wall stands and leaves the maps where they are, unless the listener ends more than about 2 km from what they show. Each map keeps its own zoom. "Reset the view" on the table or on the wall brings everything back to the site.

**Who is it good for (right wall).** The faces are a way to look at the same results from several sides, not a survey of what those people think. A group smiles when the results that touch it add up to more gained than given up (each result counts once, twice or three times with its size; one with no size yet counts once), frowns when more is given up, and keeps a straight face otherwise. The developer smiles while the choices fit its offer and frowns when they ask for more.

**Day and night (center wall).** "Time" turns the wall to night. At night the same sound counts as 10 dB more, the rule of the day-night level (DNL) used in US noise rules, which adds 10 dB between 10 pm and 7 am: the readout says what the level counts as, and more residents are shown as hearing the plant. With Listen on, a soft background plays behind the plant, as 45 dBA by day and 10 dB less at night; it is for the ear, not measured. The dark, the stars, the lamps on the campus and the mist that drifts across the immersive view are scenery.

**Sentiment map (Texas).** The left wall holds three kinds of voice, kept apart everywhere they appear. **Reported**: the eight positions reported in the press about the proposal, each with its source. **Social media, synthetic**: 72 remarks invented for this page so that the map can be read with many voices before any are collected, spread over six kinds of social media and web (X, Facebook, Reddit, Nextdoor, Instagram, a web forum), eight topics and the three positions. None is a real post and together they are not a survey; each carries the tag "Synthetic", the panel says so, and their pins (ringed) are set at random around the map's landmarks, not where anyone lives. They are written in `SYN` in `index.html` and are the first thing to replace when real remarks exist. **Sessions**: what people add in the room with "Add your voice", with the name of the session (remembered between voices) and a topic if they give one. The panel on the left chooses what is shown, by kind, by network and by topic, and turns the heat on or off; the counts and the list on the right follow. The heat is each shown voice drawn large and blurred in the color of its position.

**The right wall as a window.** Behind the simple view of the right wall there is a second view of the same ground: the listener's place at the same eye height, turned to the right by the width of one screen, so in the room the horizon and the sound rings run on from the center wall to the right wall. It follows the center wall when someone turns it, walks, changes the eye height or switches to night, also from another window. It is a backdrop: nothing on it is touched. The rings are the model's and are drawn only where the sound is computed (Texas); the sky is scenery. If the map cannot load, a drawn sky and ground stay. The bubbles, the faces and the budget float on it, with one color per chapter (design dark, technology blue, energy amber, benefits green) on the chapter keys, on the tag of every bubble and in the two budget strips, where each choice takes the part it costs. The lists say whose they are ("The neighborhood gains", "The neighborhood gives up"), and a figure about sound says where it is heard: at the "You" pin, the listener's marker on the table.

**The ground from the ground.** In the view from the ground (center wall and the backdrop of the right wall) the land is the aerial photograph (Esri World Imagery) laid over the pale map as a wash, its darks lifted and its contrast taken down: a middle point between the drawn map and the raw photograph, soft as if painted, with more haze towards the horizon. The light is a little stronger and warmer so buildings have a lit and a shaded side, and the town's buildings are a soft lilac grey, not white. Darker corners and a warm-to-cool wash are laid over it, as through a lens: scenery, like the sky. Immersive is the first person: the eye comes down to 2 m, a person's height, and goes back to the height chosen on leaving.

**Reading the right wall.** The simple view carries no title or slate: the two lists say whose they are, and each figure says what it measures (water and electricity are the plant's own use, sound is at the "You" pin on the table, money is part of the budget). The landscape is darkened only behind the words, so its middle stays clear. A tapped figure is held and can be asked three things: why, what would change it (the alternatives listed can be chosen there) and who says so. In the middle stands the budget as one bubble, after The Community Carbon Impact (Izquierdo, Bílá, Iruretagoyena and Larson, interactions 32(6), 2025): the bubble is what stays with the community out of the upfront budget, the benefits chosen as a solid core and what is not allocated yet as the paler rest around it; every choice made about the plant itself (design, technology, energy) leaves a dashed ring in the color of its chapter, named, with what it took, so the bubble is seen to have shrunk from each ring to the next. It always carries the figure: it turns amber when nothing is left to allocate, and red, with the amount asked for beyond the budget, when the choices ask for more; under it one line gives the yearly part and says what does not fit. Prices are examples. What is not known yet is not counted on this view: the questions are under Details.

**One choice, one consequence.** When a choice changes on the table, the right wall first shows that choice alone for about three seconds: its name, its strongest result set very large, who it is good for or hard on, and what it takes from the budget. Then the whole picture comes back. A tap skips it, and it is not shown where reduced motion is asked for. The wall is set in one heavy typeface (Hanken Grotesk, the one of the rest of the site), black on the landscape by day and white by night, with no dark layer over the day.

**Listening.** The sound of the plant is made in the browser and follows the "You" pin: drag the pin on the table (or walk on the center wall) and it gets louder or softer, brighter near the plant and duller far from it; change the design or the cooling and it changes again. It can be switched on from the center wall (Listen) or from the table (the loudspeaker button, Texas), and it plays in the window where it was switched on. The level on the screens is always the model's. What the loudspeaker plays follows an internal table from that level, read between its rows: 30 dBA and below, silence; 35, 0.03 of full level; 40, 0.07; 45, 0.15; 50, 0.3; 55, 0.52; 60, 0.82; 65, 1.15; 70 and above, 1.5. It is staged so that every step can be heard, with a soft background behind it only while the plant is heard, so far away is silence; it is not a measurement. On the table the pin carries the level the model gives where it stands ("You · 41 dBA"). With the reference plant (26 MW) the sound is heard only within a few hundred metres of the building; the 600 MW switch on the center wall carries it much farther.

**The right wall: one bubble, the rest in words.** There is one thing to look at, the money, and it is one bubble: the developer's offer to the community. The offer is a fixed share (20% of the profits in the brief, or $10M upfront and $0.5M a year; this session's figure can be set on the table), so the bubble is always the same size: one cannot have as much as one would like, only choose what it is made of. It is a drop of glass filled from the bottom by the four choices, each as large to the eye as its part of the money (design, technology, energy, which takes what the others leave, and benefits); each layer says its name and what it took where there is room, and what is not yet allocated stays empty glass at the top. What is in the glass has two soft colors and no more, a warm one at the bottom running to a cool one at the top, with a fine light seam where two layers meet. The wall has no row of the four choices: they are named in the bubble. When a choice changes, a black card with white words shows that one choice for three seconds; then the layers rise and fall to their new levels with a small wave, so a benefit added is seen to push the energy down: that is the trade, and the line under the headline says it in words (the last choice moved $3M from energy to benefits). If the choices ask for more than the offer the bubble turns red and the headline says by how much. The bubble is drawn live by the graphics card (a small WebGL program, lit from the upper left and seen from a little above, never quite still); where that is not to be had, a flat drawing of the same layers takes its place, and with reduced motion it is shown at rest. At rest the wall is the landscape and nothing else, with the bubble alone at its right: the room stays immersive. When the pointer is over the bubble, or it is touched, the bubble moves in and the page of words comes up beside it; it goes again when the pointer has left both for a moment, or, if it was opened by a touch, when the landscape is touched. Open, the landscape is a window on the left, where the bubble floats; behind the words it is blurred into soft fields of color under a thin blue veil that deepens gently towards the right edge, by day as by night, so the words are white on calm ground and need no glow or outline. The look is after James Turrell's As Seen Below (The Dome): the page is a field of sky blue, the bubble a body of pink light running to lilac, white at its heart, with a halo and no reflection, and the words are white in a tall serif (Instrument Serif) for the sentence and the figures, with light sans for the rest and a small spaced label over each part. The people who move about on the table and the plan are small black dots. Beside the bubble one sentence says what it is (the developer offers the community so much; each choice takes part of it, and gives something for it), and under it there is one row for every choice, in the order of the layers from top to bottom: on the left what the choice takes of the offer, on the right what it gives, each result on one line. What is given up is in the warm color of the bubble, everything else is white; a use that got less because of the last choice says so in its row (so much less than before the last choice: it went to benefits). Tapping a layer of the bubble, or the left of a row, looks at that one choice: the other layers and rows go dark, and the row says in a sentence what the choice took and from where (energy has no price of its own: it gets what the other three leave). A tapped figure is held and answers why, what would change it and who says so. Prices are examples and the wall says so.

**The left wall: a voice and its place.** Tapping a point on the map looks at that voice: its card comes into view in the list on the right, is ringed in the color of its position, and a thread is drawn from the point to the card. Tapping a card does the same from the other side, and brings its point into view if it was off the map. A tap on the bare map lets go. (Points used to open a second copy of the card over the map; they no longer do.) Each card says where the voice comes from with a small drawing: a page for a voice reported in the press, with the name of the source and a link to it where its address is known (none of the reported voices carries an address yet); a speech bubble for the synthetic sample, with the network it imitates and the plain statement that it was invented, so there is no post to link to; a table for a voice added in a session, with the session's name, which leads to the table. The panel on the left (Add your voice, and what is on the map) closes with a small ×, to see the map whole; a small tab brings it back, and while it is closed a tap on the map does not mark a place. The list of voices on the right closes the same way; a point tapped while it is away brings it back, to show its voice.

**The cone of view on the table.** At the "You" pin the table shows a cone: the way the center wall looks from there, and as wide as that wall shows (about 61 degrees across, roughly the central field of a person's sight; the whole field of two eyes is about twice as wide). A round handle at its far end turns it, and the center wall, and the landscape of the right wall beside it, turn with it, in the same window or another. Turning the center wall by hand turns the cone. The cone is drawn 240 m long whatever the zoom, so it looks short when the map is zoomed far out.

**Ribbons from the bubble to the plant.** A choice that changes the plant itself (its design, its technology) can be tied to the physical model: touching that layer of the bubble, or its row, draws a ribbon in the layer's color, with a curl on the way, from the layer to the plant in the landscape. Where the plant is not in the right wall's own picture, the ribbon runs to the edge it lies beyond and the center wall carries it on from its own edge to the plant, at the height the right wall's ribbon left (the two walls tell each other through the browser's storage). Touching the layer again takes the ribbon away; choices that are not a thing on the site (energy bought elsewhere, benefits) have none. `?ribbon=0` turns them off altogether. The ribbon ends at the plant's place on the ground, not at the particular wall, tree or cooler the choice adds. At rest the page opens for a pointer that stays on the bubble about a third of a second, so a layer can be tapped before the bubble moves; a tap opens the page and looks at the layer tapped.

**Moving and turning the data center.** On the table the building can be dragged by itself or by the handle with four arrows beside it, and turned with the round handle. At a site known only by its size (College Station, drawn as a circle of about 200 acres) it may stand anywhere on that ground, as long as its middle keeps 60 m inside the circle, and face any way: the turn handle is carried round the building. At a site with a parcel line it keeps to the parcel and to 15 degrees either way, as before. The sound rings, the figure at the "You" pin and the walls follow when it is let go, and the center wall faces the plant where it now stands. Choosing the conventional campus puts it back where it began.

**Moving about in the view from the ground.** Besides tapping the ground to walk there and dragging to look around, the arrow keys or W and S walk ahead and back, A and D step aside without turning, Q and E (or the left and right arrows) turn, and Shift goes three times as far; the buttons at the foot of the wall have a step to the left and to the right as well. Where one stands is the "You" pin of the table.

**A control panel that keeps out of the way.** The panel of the center wall is a ghost until the pointer is over it or it is touched: frosted glass with only its name, so the view stays whole. The button in its corner folds it to a small tab; the tab opens while the pointer is over it, and a tap unfolds it for good. The bubble of the right wall likewise lets the landscape through while nobody is at it, and a little even when open; it is never still: its levels roll slowly and the light inside it wanders.

**The film.** Adding `?film=1` to the address makes the tool play itself through one story, to be recorded from the screen: a card with a Start button, then about a minute and forty seconds through the four screens (the place at a person's height, the "You" pin on the table and what is heard there, day to night, two choices, the bubble and its ribbon, a voice added, the four screens together) and a closing card. The click on Start is what lets the sound begin and the page fill the screen. The narration is shown under the picture; `&subs=0` leaves it out. The pointer, the page's header and the control panel are hidden while it plays, the people who walk about are off, the figures shown are the tool's own, and the sentence typed as a voice is an example that is not kept afterwards.

**The film in parts.** `?film=1&part=1` to `&part=5` play one section each, about half a minute, with a drawn pointer that goes to each thing and presses it, and nothing written under the picture: 1 the voices (two are read on the map, the list is filtered, then a voice is placed, written and added), 2 the scenario on the table (a choice in each of the four dimensions), 3 the place, for the ear (down to a person's height, towards the plant and away from it, then the night, with the small readout of the sound), 4 what it would mean (the bubble at rest, then layer by layer what each choice takes of the offer and what it gives), 5 the four screens together (choices made on the table change the walls, and so does moving the "You" pin). In the parts the thread of the left wall and the ribbons are left out, and the control panel of the center wall stays a ghost until the pointer is on it.

**The first person, without the measure.** In Immersive the sound rings, their tint on the ground and their labels are put away: only the place is seen. What is heard is told by one small readout at the bottom left: five bars that move when Listen is on, the level where you stand, and whether you would hear the plant from there. With the panels hidden (H) the marks of the tour are hidden too. H also clears the view from the ground outside Immersive: the panel, the buttons and the two name labels go, and the rings keep their dBA. The small readout of the sound takes the panel's place, so a cleared view still says what is heard. The landscape behind the right wall no longer carries the rings either, since nothing on that wall names them.

**A softer plant.** Planted trees have round crowns (fourteen thin tiers on a slim trunk) instead of six-sided blocks, tanks are round, the plant's small parts are paler and the light is softer, so the model reads less as a row of cubes. The sound rings are a thin violet line with a faint wash, in the color of the other screens.

**A softer ground.** From the ground the land is the drawn map, flat and pale, with the aerial photograph laid over it as a blurred wash at under half its strength, and the whole scene is in slightly soft focus. Labels and panels stay sharp.

**The painted look.** From the ground the center wall stays in three dimensions but is seen as through a painting: the aerial photograph comes forward, and the whole drawing is moved a little, as by a brush, and diffused. The landscape behind the right wall is painted the same way. It is drawn by a filter over the map, so the text, the labels and the bubble stay sharp. The haze on the horizon has no edge of its own: it lies soft across the line where the ground ends, so the ground is not cut by a white line. `?paint=0` in the address leaves it out.

**The reveal.** When a choice arrives on the right wall, the card that names it is now set as the rest of that wall: the serif, no capitals, and the place still seen through it.

**One look for the four screens.** The look chosen is **c**, pale painted paper with lilac, and it is what the tool opens with. The other three sketches are still there to compare: add `?look=a`, `?look=b` or `?look=d` to the address (remembered for every window of the tool), `?look=c` to come back, and `?look=0` to see the tool with no look at all. A look sets the same few things on all four screens: the ground, the surfaces, the ink, one accent, the face of headings, and the bubble's two colors. **a** is sky blue with pink light and a tall serif (after James Turrell); **b** is dark steel blue with thin type (after Onionlab's The Outcome); **c** is pale painted paper with lilac; **d** is black with heavy white type. The maps themselves keep their colors in every look, and in the two dark looks (b and d) some switches and chips still have their light backgrounds: they are sketches to choose from, not finished.

**Closing a scenario.** On the right wall, "Close this scenario as agreed" keeps the scenario with what it gains, gives up and leaves unknown. It then appears on the sentiment map (Texas), where people can say what they think of it; their answers are counted on the closed scenario on both walls. A closed scenario can be shown again or reopened.

**Process** (`#examples`; the tab used to be called Examples): at the top, the room with the tool as it is now, made by placing real captures of the four screens onto the walls and table of a generated image of the room and its people; then the annotated captures of the four screens, and steps on the way, the first picture of the room among them.

**Access code.** The page asks for a code every time it is opened, before it shows anything; nothing is remembered on the device (only the reload the page does by itself when a new version is published passes without asking again). The code is set in `access.txt`, which holds its SHA-256; it is off while that file is empty. To change it, open the page with `?setcode`, type the new code, and put the line it gives in `access.txt`. This keeps casual visitors out; it is not security, because the code of the page is public.

A scenario can be given in the address: `?site=texas&pick=D2,T2,E1,B1,B4&ch=design`.

## Case studies

The switch in the header (or `?site=texas` / `?site=michigan` in the address) changes the case study on every screen.

- **Michigan** — Allen Park, the site of the original DataScope.
- **Texas** — College Station: about 200 acres of city land in the Midtown Business Park, at Midtown Drive and Corporate Parkway, which the city council declined to sell for a 600 MW data center on 11 September 2025 (reported by KBTX and KXXV). The tract's outline is not drawn; the map shows the crossing the press reports and a circle of the same area, set on the undeveloped land beside it, to give its size. Landmark positions come from Google Maps. With no original tool for this site, the center wall shows the aerial photo and the left wall a sentiment map of its own: positions reported in the press about the proposal (KBTX, KXXV, Chron, BCS Chronicle, TexAgs), each with its source and set around the site, not at anyone's home; a synthetic sample of social media remarks, marked as synthetic (see Sentiment map above); and a window left open where the people in the room add their voice in a session: a position, one sentence, and marks tapped on the map (one place, several places linked, or none for the list only). They can have a picture drawn from the sentence; for that the sentence alone is sent to an open image service (Pollinations). Everything else added in sessions is kept in that browser only (Export sessions downloads it as a file; Clear sessions removes it; the synthetic sample and the reported voices are never exported or cleared). The figures on the right wall are still those of the Michigan reference building (26 MW), not of the 600 MW proposal.

## Where the figures come from

Only figures with a source are shown.

- **Cooling packages** (electricity, peak demand, cooling water, sound): the three designs of the original DataScope Scenario Editor (`dist/studio/analysis.js`: Reference, Dry cooling, Higher efficiency), for 17.3 MW of computing in Southeast Michigan. T2 uses Dry cooling, T3 uses Higher efficiency.
- **Energy**: the cost and land to match all the power, from the project notes ($275M solar, $150M wind, about $1B for six microreactors).
- **The budget**: what the developer offers the community to allocate. It is set for the session at $20M upfront and $1M a year; the brief's own offer is $10M upfront and $0.5M a year, or 20% of the profits. On the table, **+ $1M** and **+ $0.1M** add to it one tap at a time, the table says what has come within reach, and the ↺ button brings back the starting budget; any figure can also be typed under Prices.
- **Prices of the alternatives: examples, not the developer's.** The brief asks to show the cost of each alternative but gives no figure for designs, cooling packages or benefits. So that choosing spends against the budget, each has an example price (upfront and a year) written in the page (`PRICE0`), named as an example on the table and on the walls, and open to change on the table. The baselines cost nothing extra. Replace them when real prices are known.
- **How the budget works**: choosing an alternative spends its price; an alternative that does not fit what is left cannot be chosen, on the table, on the right wall or by voice. The four benefits together cost more than the budget, so not all can be chosen. An energy alternative has no price of its own: it takes whatever the other choices leave of the upfront money, which buys part of the power; its wedge on the wheel is as long as that share (under 10%, 10 to 30%, over 30%).
- **Designs and benefits**: the brief describes them without measurements, so their direction is shown and their size is listed under "Not known yet".
- **Words for sizes** use the same steps every time: a little (under 10%), clearly (10 to 30%), much (over 30%); for sound, under 3 dB, 3 to 8 dB, over 8 dB.
- **Sound where you stand** (Texas contours, residents, listening): the method the original names, ISO 9613-2, kept simple. The plant's two sources (rooftop units and west yard) have the levels the original gives for each cooling package at 15 m. Each loses 6 dB for each doubling of the distance and 0.0035 dB per metre to the air. A wall, a berm or a building in the line of sight takes off 10·lg(3 + 20·z/λ), at most 20 dB (z: the detour over the top; λ: 1.37 m, as in the original). Dense trees take off 1 dB for 10 to 20 m of them and 0.04 dB per metre beyond, the standard's values near 250 Hz, **quoted from memory and to be checked against the standard**. Ground, wind and the ends of a wall are not counted. Wall and berm heights are not in the brief: they are the ones set on the table. The 600 MW switch is a what-if: 23 reference plants heard as one source (+14 dB).
- **Residents**: 2020 Census blocks (TIGERweb, field POP100).
- The sound you hear is made in the browser. Its loudness follows the model; its color is an impression, not a recording.
